Berlin vs Hamburg, like for like

Chapter 3 — The Evidence

Berlin vs Hamburg, like for like

Compared like for like (neighbourhoods of similar size, the same three indicators of poverty and unemployment, the same years and the same map data), Berlin and Hamburg show no detectable difference in how the local mix of shops and services relates to neighbourhood disadvantage. The comparison can only pick up large differences, so this does not mean the two cities are the same.

The Berlin and Hamburg re-checks of the 2018 thesis each use their own city's monitor, area size and years, so they cannot be set against each other directly. This page puts the two cities on one footing instead: areas of similar size, the same three indicators, the same editions and the same map data. It then tests, hypothesis by hypothesis, whether the link between the local business mix and neighbourhood disadvantage shows a detectable city difference.

What the comparison shows

  • In each city, areas where cafés make up a larger share of local businesses tend to be less disadvantaged, and areas where fast-food outlets do tend to be more disadvantaged. This describes where businesses are today. It does not mean that cafés improve an area or that fast-food outlets signal a problem: which businesses can survive depends largely on local incomes, rents and passing trade.
  • In neither city did changes in the business mix come before changes in measured disadvantage, or follow them, between 2019 and 2025. The areas compared have 15,000–26,000 residents, so changes at street or block level may not show up at this scale.
  • In Berlin, the business mix gave a weak hint of which areas fell behind in 2021–2023 only. It disappears without the welfare-receipt indicator and adds nothing to knowing how disadvantaged an area already was. That period coincides with refugees from Ukraine entering basic income support (Grundsicherung), so we do not read it as an effect of local businesses.
  • The years covered include the pandemic, the arrival of refugees from Ukraine and the 2023 benefit reforms. These moved the official indicators in both cities, but not always through the same indicator or at the same time.
  • These results describe patterns in official statistics and open map data. They are not forecasts and cannot be used to identify neighbourhoods that will gentrify.

Read this first

  • Welfare receipt. Welfare receipt is measured slightly differently in the two cities: Berlin's figure, derived from two published indicators, includes social assistance (mainly older and disabled people); Hamburg's includes asylum-seeker benefits. A city difference is only stated if it also appears without this indicator.
  • Refugee accommodation in Hamburg. In Hamburg, the welfare indicator includes asylum-seeker benefits. Where the city opens or closes refugee accommodation, an area's figure can change by tens of percentage points without any change among its existing residents. Such changes show where accommodation was placed. We do not read them as neighbourhood decline or improvement.
  • Scale. The matched units (15–26k residents) are coarser than the scale at which gentrification is usually observed, so a null here does not rule out a neighbourhood-scale process.
  • Within-city scores. The disadvantage score is standardised within each city, so it ranks areas within a city only. A Berlin and a Hamburg area with the same score are not equally disadvantaged, and this page does not compare the cities' levels.
  • Change is not either city's official trend. The change measure here is built from the three shared indicators. It is not either city's own published change classification.

How the comparison works

  • Matched grain. Berlin Bezirksregionen (BZR, 143) and Hamburg Stadtteile (99): areas of roughly 15,000–26,000 residents and 4–6 km². Each is built up from the city's smaller areas (Berlin planning areas, Hamburg statistical areas), weighted by residents.
  • Harmonised core. The same three indicators in both cities: unemployment, welfare receipt and child poverty. Berlin's welfare figure is re-based to all recipients (T*), so that both cities weigh unemployment the same way. The remaining differences in what each figure counts are disclosed above.
  • Same years. The 2019, 2021, 2023 and 2025 editions of each city's social monitor, with the same reference dates and three two-year windows, and the same OpenStreetMap snapshots and business categories. Berlin's end-2022 population, used as weights, is interpolated between 2020 and 2024, as fixed in advance.
  • Spatial block bootstrap. Confidence intervals resample 24 blocks of neighbouring areas per city (1,999 draws), so neighbours are not treated as independent. Benjamini–Hochberg corrects across each city's eight tests and across the eight city comparisons.
  • Hamburg 2019 base revised by the publisher. Hamburg's 2019 welfare figures come from an S3 base revised by the publisher (a change in how asylum-seeker benefits are counted, Hamburg Sozialmonitoring report 2021). The comparison uses the revised base; the figures as first published are a check.
  • Fixed in advance. The design, the checks and this page's statement rule were registered before the results were computed. Two clarifications (how a check is marked as moving a result, and how the 5,000-resident condition of the statement rule is tested) were added after the results were computed and are disclosed in Amendment 5 of the pre-registration: pre-registration, data checks, findings.

The thesis hypotheses in each city

Each city is tested on its own, with the same design. A result counts as supported or opposite only if it is significant after a Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the city's eight tests. ρ is a rank correlation with the disadvantage score (positive: goes with more disadvantage); AUC says how well the mix tells areas apart (0.5 is chance). Brackets give the 95% confidence interval.
No Results

Berlin H2. In Berlin, the business mix gave a weak hint of which areas fell behind in 2021–2023 only. It disappears without the welfare-receipt indicator and adds nothing to knowing how disadvantaged an area already was. That period coincides with refugees from Ukraine entering basic income support (Grundsicherung), so we do not read it as an effect of local businesses.

Berlin H1c, read carefully. In Berlin, where sport and leisure make up a larger share of all mapped places, measured disadvantage tends to be higher. That share rises where few shops and services are mapped, so the result is about the mix of mapped places, not about sports provision as such.

City differences, tested

For each hypothesis, the test compares Berlin's estimate with Hamburg's: Δ is Berlin's minus Hamburg's. The MDD is the smallest difference the comparison could reliably detect (2.8 times its standard error). "No difference detected" is never "the same": where the MDD is larger than 0.30 (ρ) or 0.10 (AUC), the comparison is too imprecise to say the cities agree.
No Results

Statement rule, fixed in advance. A city difference is stated only if it shows up in this main test, holds up without areas under 5,000 residents and without the welfare-receipt indicator, and points the same way with Berlin's uncorrected welfare indicator. None of the eight comparisons gives a difference in the main test, so no city difference is stated.

H2, 2021–2023 window only (exploratory). Run on this window alone, without correction for multiple testing, the comparison test is flagged. That is not a city difference: in this window Hamburg's estimate is below chance, so the test only registers Berlin's weak 2021–2023 hint (see Berlin H2 above) against nothing usable on the Hamburg side (pre-registration Amendment 3, D6). The window coincides with refugees from Ukraine entering basic income support in June 2022, which did not enter both cities' indicators through the same route. The main comparison, over all three windows, detects no difference.

Three new hypotheses

Pre-registered for this comparison: N1 and N2 are confirmatory in each city (corrected across the city's N tests), N3 is exploratory, and the city differences for N1–N3 are exploratory and uncorrected. These are ordering tests, not warning, forecasting or investment indicators.
No Results

N1 is about the more disadvantaged half of neighbourhoods in each city, which mixes very different kinds of area; it is not about gentrifying areas.

In Berlin, N1 could not be tested at this grain: the areas above the median fall into too few of the spatial blocks used for the confidence intervals.

Reading the checks. Each result was also re-run with one change at a time. A check is listed under a result only where it changed that result's sign or its significance before correction. Sign unstable around zero: the estimate changes sign, but is not statistically significant in either version, so it is not a substantive change. Unadjusted significance change: significant before correction in one version and not in the other. Grain-dependent, MAUP: the result depends on the size of the areas (the modifiable areal unit problem). No check can change a verdict, and a check is not a finding on its own. Berlin's uncorrected welfare indicator, for example, leaves out recipients who are unemployed, while Hamburg's includes them, so the two cities' scores would weigh unemployment differently. That is why the corrected one is used for every verdict.

The tables are read directly from the committed results table (run af2312b5c72c).

Honest caveats

  • With about 100–143 areas per city, the comparison can detect only large differences between the cities.
  • OpenStreetMap is not mapped equally completely everywhere, nor equally in both cities, so the business mix is captured more fully in some areas than in others.
  • All analysis is at the level of areas. Nothing here says anything about an individual person, household, business or building.
  • These are associations. Nothing here shows that commercial change causes change in officially measured disadvantage.
  • The many secondary and sensitivity checks in the findings are not corrected for multiple testing, so they are context, not verdicts.
