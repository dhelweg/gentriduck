The 2018 thesis, re-checked

The historical reproduction study

The 2018 thesis, re-checked

Gentriduck began as a question: an award-era master's thesis from 2018 claimed that the churn of shops, cafés and restaurants in a Berlin neighbourhood tracks — and partly predicts — its social change. Eight years and a completely rebuilt, open-source stack later: does that result still hold?

This page answers that hypothesis by hypothesis. It is deliberately about the reproduction — the historical check. For what Berlin looks like today, start with the home page, the maps, and the area detail; those lead with the current data, where these 2018 hypotheses matter less than the live picture.

The short version: partly. In 2026 we re-tested all eight of the thesis's hypotheses with a design fixed in advance, once on the thesis's own era (2013–2019) and once on 2019–2025. Two of its findings hold in both: an area's social status is linked to its local business mix, modestly (H1), and fast food makes up a larger share of the gastronomy mix where officially measured disadvantage is higher (H1b). On timing, we find no detectable ordering between commercial change and change in officially measured disadvantage, in either direction (H3a–c): the thesis's tentative finding that a fall in disadvantage comes first (H3b) is not reproduced, and its rejection of "commerce first" (H3a) holds. The link between today's mix and the next change (H2) is explained by an area's current status level, which matches how the thesis itself read it. One result this page used to report, that a fall in disadvantage came before commercial change on recent data, is not reproduced; see the corrections below.

The original thesis repository is left untouched as the historical record; Gentriduck is a from-scratch rebuild from open sources only. Two methodological points matter here: the thesis blended everything into one score, while this project keeps what we're trying to explain (social status) strictly separate from the commercial signal used to explain it (see the methodology page); and this re-check uses the thesis's actual predictor for the status hypotheses (H1, H2) — the Offering Advantage, a location quotient of how over- or under-represented a category of shops, services or public facilities is in a given area, as the thesis computed it — rather than a plain POI count. The timing hypotheses (H3a–c) use growth in commercial businesses only (shops, gastronomy, services, entertainment).

The eight hypotheses, then and now

How the re-test works. Every hypothesis was re-tested in 2026 with a design fixed in advance (pre-registered), in two periods: the thesis era (2013–2019), re-analysed with current methods, and 2019–2025. Each hypothesis has one main test per period. A result counts only if it is statistically significant after a Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the eight main tests of its period: within a period, this bounds the expected share of false claims among the results marked significant; it does not protect each result individually. Confidence intervals resample whole district regions (Bezirksregionen), so neighbouring planning areas are not treated as independent. The timing tests use two starting points per period, allow for an area's starting level, and count commercial businesses only.

What "status" means here. Social status as measured by Berlin's Monitoring Soziale Stadtentwicklung: unemployment, welfare receipt and child poverty. For change we say "change in officially measured disadvantage". It can fall for many reasons besides gentrification: jobs, children growing older, new-build residents, refugee accommodation closing, or benefit rule changes. Our change measure is a continuous score built from the same three indicators. Its rank agreement with the official three-class Dynamik ordering is about 0.7, which is 95–100% of the highest agreement any continuous score can reach, because most areas share one class. That also means the 0.8 agreement we set in advance could not be reached in any edition and was missed. Where the score and the classes disagree, we report both.

  • Not like-for-like. The two periods use different planning-area boundaries (before and after Berlin's 2021 redraw, so no area is followed across it), different status measures for H1–H1c (the thesis's own December 2016 status index; a composite of the 2025 monitor), indicator definitions that changed in between, and different OpenStreetMap completeness. Corrections

Correction (November 2026, #354). Earlier versions of this page reported that, on Berlin's social monitor for 2021–2025, a fall in officially measured disadvantage came before commercial change (H3b). That came from a simpler test: one time window, no allowance for an area's starting level, and all mapped objects rather than businesses. A re-test whose design was fixed in advance does not reproduce it, for 2021–2025 or for the thesis era. We now report H3b as "not reproduced".

The same re-test changes two rows in the other direction. H1 now holds: earlier versions reported it as "not reproduced by a single-number proxy", because the thesis's multi-feature test had not been re-run. H2 on the 2021–2025 monitor moves from "supported" to "explained by current status level". Earlier editions of this page also called the overall result a clean replication; the October 2026 entry below explains why that was wrong.

Earlier corrections, kept for the record:

  • Method correction (October 2026). Earlier editions reported H3a and H3b from a test that computed the same correlation for both directions, so it could never tell them apart, and counted a result as "passing" whenever its sign matched, even when it was indistinguishable from noise. The test was changed to a cross-lagged design that requires significance. The thesis's own verdicts were also misreported here: it found H2 and H3c inconclusive, rejected H3a, and supported H3b only tentatively (thesis Tabelle 6-16).
  • Data correction (October 2026). Shops and services deleted from OpenStreetMap had been kept in every later year by an extraction bug, inflating counts by up to about a fifth and making commercial growth look stronger than it was. All results here use the corrected data.
  • Data correction (2026-07-09, #200): the H1 (OA) test's sample was previously understated (n=92 instead of the correct n=435) by an area-code join bug that silently dropped most PLRs; fixing it changed the H1 (OA) result from "not significant either way" to "statistically significant, in the direction opposite the thesis's prior" — a more decisive, not a more favourable, finding. No other hypothesis in this table was affected. (After the October 2026 deletion fix the sample is n=433.) That single-number test is no longer how H1 is tested; see the November 2026 entry above.

What still matters for social science today

For the current picture these hypotheses take a back seat to the live typology on the home page. But three threads remain genuinely interesting:

  • Fast food's share of the gastronomy mix (H1b). In both periods, among neighbourhoods with at least ten gastronomy outlets, fast food makes up a larger share of the gastronomy mix where officially measured disadvantage is higher, a moderate effect. It is an area-level pattern, read as a deprivation hypothesis, not a statement about any outlet or its owners; the number of fast-food outlets per resident shows no clear link.
  • The timing question (H3a–c). With a design that separates the two directions, we find no detectable ordering in either direction, in either era. That rules out a strong ordering, not a small one, and every window contains shocks and rule changes.
  • The thesis's own caution about H2. An area's current level predicts the next change in officially measured disadvantage better on its own, and adding the mix of shops, services and public facilities adds nothing. This matches the 2018 thesis, which read its own H2 association as a spurious correlation running through the current status level.

Honest caveats

  • Significance is corrected across the eight main tests of each period (Benjamini–Hochberg). The many secondary and sensitivity checks in the findings are not corrected: of about 90 such checks, about 4.5 would come out significant by chance alone, so they are context, not verdicts.
  • All analysis is at the aggregate planning-area level. Nothing here says anything about an individual person, household, or building (the ecological fallacy).
  • These are associations. Nothing here shows that commercial change causes change in officially measured disadvantage, or that anyone was displaced.
  • MSS and OSM measure different things on different schedules; a difference between them is a measurement story as much as a real-world one.
  • Every result counts places mapped as points in OpenStreetMap; places mapped as building outlines or areas are not yet counted (#337), and mapping completeness grew over both periods.
  • There is no open small-area source on refugee accommodation, so its effect on the benefit-based indicators is disclosed, not corrected.
  • The resident-register (EWR) panel is outside this re-test. Its earlier results remain documented in the E1 regression findings, §4.
  • Reproducing a 2018 result is a directional check, not a number-for-number replay — see the Epic B framing for why.
