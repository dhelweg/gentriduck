The 2018 thesis, re-checked

The historical reproduction study

The 2018 thesis, re-checked

Gentriduck began as a question: an award-era master's thesis from 2018 claimed that the churn of shops, cafés and restaurants in a Berlin neighbourhood tracks — and partly predicts — its social change. Eight years and a completely rebuilt, open-source stack later: does that result still hold?

This page answers that hypothesis by hypothesis. It is deliberately about the reproduction — the historical check. For what Berlin looks like today, start with the home page, the maps, and the area detail; those lead with the current data, where these 2018 hypotheses matter less than the live picture.

The short version: partly. The thesis's clearest result — fast-food density goes with lower social status (H1b) — replicates robustly, and most strongly with the thesis's own commercial predictor, the Offering Advantage. Its timing claim — social change comes before commercial change (H3b), which the thesis itself rated only "rather confirmed" — holds on Berlin's official social monitor for 2021–2025, but not on its 2013–2019 editions. Those include the thesis's own 2013, 2015 and 2017 editions, so they are the like-for-like re-test, and there H2, H3a and H3b are all not significant — though the re-check measures change as a move between the four status classes, while the thesis used the finer MSS Dynamik index, so this is a lower-powered test, not a clean refutation. On Berlin's resident register (EWR), which measures population composition rather than social status, commerce and composition move together with neither clearly leading. The thesis's claim that social status is related to the local mix of businesses (H1) was only re-tested with single-number proxies, which do not reproduce it. The relationship between commerce and social change is real, but fragile — it depends on how you measure both and on the period you look at. Earlier editions of this page called it a clean replication; see the caveats for why that was wrong.

The original thesis repository is left untouched as the historical record; Gentriduck is a from-scratch rebuild from open sources only. Two methodological points matter here: the thesis blended everything into one score, while this project keeps what we're trying to explain (social status) strictly separate from the commercial signal used to explain it (see the methodology page); and this re-check (Run 1, faithful revival) now uses the thesis's actual predictor — the Offering Advantage, a location quotient of how over- or under-represented a business category is in a given area, exactly as the thesis computed it (its oa_*/prev_oa_* columns) — rather than a plain POI count. Both are shown below where they diverge.

The six hypotheses, then and now

Each hypothesis links commercial activity to social status. A result only counts if it is statistically significant — not merely pointing the expected way. Supported = significant in the thesis's predicted direction; contradicted = significant in the opposite direction; not significant = the data cannot tell. H3a and H3b are claims about timing, so they are tested with a cross-lagged design: changes in two strictly ordered, non-overlapping time windows, each direction controlling for the other series' own earlier change, with confidence intervals that allow for neighbouring areas not being independent. The thesis's own verdicts are taken from its summary table (Tabelle 6-16).

Read the columns together, not alone

The verdicts hinge on two things at once: which data measures "social status," and which predictor measures "commerce."

  • On Berlin's official social monitor, thesis era (MSS, editions 2013–2019): this includes the thesis's own 2013, 2015 and 2017 editions, so it is the like-for-like re-test. H2, H3a and H3b are all not significant, under either predictor. But the re-check measures change as a move between the four status classes, while the thesis used the finer MSS Dynamik index, so this is a lower-powered test, not a clean refutation.
  • On Berlin's resident register (EWR, 2014–2020, raw POI count): this series measures population composition (residence duration, age structure, nationality / migration background), not social status, and it was not the thesis's outcome. Both timing directions are significant at similar strength — at the two-year lag commerce-first is, if anything, the larger — so commerce and composition move together without one clearly leading. This panel uses raw OSM count changes without the completeness correction, so mapping growth may contribute to both directions. Effects are small (at most about 4% of the variation explained), and it has not yet been re-tested with the OA predictor (a documented scope boundary — see caveats).
  • On the official monitor, modern era (2021–2025): fast food (H1b) and the predictive link (H2) hold, and the timing test favours the thesis's direction — status change first (H3b), on both predictors, with commerce-first (H3a) not supported. With three editions this is a single comparison window (pivot 2023); this panel's test runs without the baseline-susceptibility (D4) control, which is unavailable at the 2023 pivot, and its windows span the pandemic and its aftermath. Whether it reflects a real shift, a better-measured outcome, the type of area, a period-specific shock or chance cannot be settled from this data alone. The MSS status index tracks concentrations of disadvantage, so "status first" here means a fall in measured disadvantage comes before commercial change — it is not evidence that anyone was displaced.
  • In Hamburg (annual Sozialmonitoring, an independent re-test), neither timing direction shows up at any lag from one to three years.

What still matters for social science today

For the current picture the six hypotheses take a back seat to the live typology on the home page. But three threads remain genuinely interesting:

  • Fast food as a durable down-signal, strengthened by the thesis's own predictor (H1b). Of everything tested, the fast-food association is the most robust — it holds on official data, it survives aggregation to Bezirksregion scale, and it gets clearly stronger once measured the way the thesis actually measured it (a location quotient, not a raw count). This is the one commercial indicator that behaves consistently as a status marker.
  • The timing question is open, not settled (H3a/H3b). With a test that actually separates the two directions, "status first, commerce second" shows up on Berlin's official monitor for 2021–2025, but not for 2013–2019 (the thesis's own editions), not in Hamburg, and on the resident-register composition series the two move together with neither leading. That is weaker than the thesis's own tentative verdict suggested, and much weaker than the "clean replication" earlier editions of this page reported.
  • Same-time co-movement (H3c) is not supported. On the official monitor it is not significant on the dynamism measure and points the other way on OA. The one significant result, on the resident register, correlates same-year levels rather than co-occurring changes, so it is not a like-for-like test of H3c.

Honest caveats

  • No multiple-comparison correction was applied; these are directional indicators, consistent with a hypothesis, not confirmatory proof. Across the roughly 40 tests behind this table, about two would come out significant by chance alone, so a single isolated result is weak evidence.
  • All analysis is at the aggregate planning-area level. Nothing here says anything about an individual person, household, or building (the ecological fallacy).
  • EWR, MSS, and OSM measure different things on different schedules; a difference between the two social measures is a measurement story as much as a real-world one.
  • The EWR panel (resident register, 2014–2020) has not yet been re-tested with the OA predictor — it still uses the raw POI count/dynamism measures from earlier editions of this page. This is a documented scope boundary for Run 1 (OA is currently built at PLR scale only), not a result — a future run may close this gap.
  • Method correction (October 2026). Earlier editions reported H3a and H3b from a test that computed the same correlation for both directions, so it could never tell them apart, and counted a result as "passing" whenever its sign matched, even when it was indistinguishable from noise. The table now uses a cross-lagged design and requires significance. The thesis's own verdicts were also misreported here: it found H2 and H3c inconclusive, rejected H3a, and supported H3b only tentatively (thesis Tabelle 6-16).
  • Data correction (October 2026). Shops and services deleted from OpenStreetMap had been kept in every later year by an extraction bug, inflating counts by up to about a fifth and making commercial growth look stronger than it was. All results here use the corrected data.
  • Reproducing a 2018 result is a directional check, not a number-for-number replay — see the Epic B framing for why.
  • Data correction (2026-07-09, #200): the H1 (OA) test's sample was previously understated (n=92 instead of the correct n=435) by an area-code join bug that silently dropped most PLRs; fixing it changed the H1 (OA) result from "not significant either way" to "statistically significant, in the direction opposite the thesis's prior" — a more decisive, not a more favourable, finding. No other hypothesis in this table was affected. (After the October 2026 deletion fix the sample is n=433.)
