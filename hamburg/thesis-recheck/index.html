Chapter 3 — The Evidence

The 2018 thesis, re-tested on Hamburg

The eight hypotheses of the 2018 Berlin thesis, re-tested on Hamburg with the same design fixed in advance, and set side by side with the thesis and with Berlin today. This is an out-of-sample transfer test, not a replication.

This page is the Hamburg counterpart of the 2018 thesis, re-checked, which explains how the re-test works and reports Berlin in full. Here every hypothesis was re-tested on Hamburg's statistische Gebiete twice: over the full chain of Sozialmonitoring editions (2013–2025, the main test) and over 2019–2025 only (pivot 2022, an era-matched comparison with its own correction, not a second confirmation).

Read this first

Hamburg measures social status differently. Its Sozialmonitoring combines seven indicators: the share of children with a migration background, children of single parents, SGB II and asylum-seeker benefits, unemployment, children and older people on minimum income, and school-leaving outcomes. It publishes four status classes and three change classes. A Hamburg result is an association with that composite, not with any one indicator or group. Hamburg is an out-of-sample transfer test, not a replication. A matching result is not evidence that the same process is at work, and a different result is not evidence that it isn't. At Hamburg's small statistical areas (about 2,200 residents), shops and services are too sparse to measure the local mix, so Hamburg's H1 and H2 use transport, public facilities and tourist facilities instead (H2: transport and public facilities only).

The labels, the estimates and the Berlin column are read directly from the committed results table (Hamburg run 1ed46268d3ed , Berlin run 05ed2ad419d4 ); AUC and ρ are the main test's estimate. The thesis column shows words only: the thesis's AUCs come from coarser areas and a different validation scheme, so they are not set against the re-test's.

Reading the labels. Holds: the re-test finds the thesis's supported result. Stronger than thesis: the thesis was undecided, and the re-test finds a significant link in the hypothesised direction. Opposite direction: a significant link the other way from the hypothesis, which the thesis had left undecided. Still unclear: the thesis was undecided, and the re-test cannot decide either. Rejected, as in 2018: the thesis rejected it, and the re-test finds no effect either. Not reproduced: the thesis's tentative support does not show up. Not comparable: the Hamburg test cannot measure what the thesis tested. Same test: the H1 family uses one 2025 cross-section in both Hamburg columns, so it is shown once and counts once.

Not like-for-like

  • Different monitor. Hamburg's seven indicators include migration background and school leaving; Berlin's status measure here is built from unemployment, welfare receipt and child poverty.
  • Different change window. Hamburg's change classes cover three years, Berlin's two.
  • Different status measure. Hamburg publishes classes; Berlin's re-test uses continuous composites.
  • Different grain. A Hamburg statistisches Gebiet has about 2,200 residents, a Berlin planning area about 7,000.
  • Different era. The Hamburg full chain covers 2013–2025, Berlin today 2019–2025; the Hamburg 2019–2025 column is the era-matched comparison.

A separate comparison puts both cities on matched areas of similar size, the same three indicators and the same years: Berlin vs Hamburg, like for like.

Corrections

This replaces an earlier Hamburg timing analysis (E5), which compared overlapping change windows. Its conclusion, no ordering in either direction, is unchanged; its numbers should no longer be cited.

Honest caveats

  • Significance is corrected across the eight main tests of each Hamburg column (Benjamini–Hochberg); the full chain is the main test, and the 2019–2025 column is a comparison, not a second confirmation. The many secondary checks in the Hamburg findings are not corrected, so they are context, not verdicts.
  • All analysis is at the aggregate level of the statistisches Gebiet. Nothing here says anything about an individual person, household, or building.
  • These are associations. Nothing here shows that commercial change causes change in officially measured disadvantage, or that anyone was displaced.
  • A fall in officially measured disadvantage is not the same as gentrification: it can come from jobs, children growing older, new-build residents, refugee accommodation closing, or benefit rule changes.
  • Every result counts places mapped as points in OpenStreetMap, and mapping completeness grew over the period.
